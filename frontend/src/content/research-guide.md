# Research Track: newcomer to research scientist

Research Track turns iReliabee into a research apprenticeship: seven levels from "what is a paper?" to defending original work, about 10 hours a week for 9 to 12 months. It wins on method, not talent: retrieval practice instead of rereading, a replication before any original idea, and daily speaking drills against AI versions of a supervisor, a reviewer and an examining committee.

## How it beats the normal route

The usual route is slow because it is passive: read a lot, wait for a supervisor's time, write late, speak rarely. Research Track replaces each slow habit with one the evidence supports.

| Normal route | Research Track | Why it is faster |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reread papers and highlight | Every paper becomes 5 to 10 retrieval cards, reviewed on a spaced schedule | Practice testing and spaced practice were the two highest-utility techniques in Dunlosky et al. (2013); rereading and highlighting were rated low |
| Wait a year to do "original" work | Replicate one published result by month 4, then extend it | You learn the real methods, data and failure modes of a field, and you end up with something to show |
| Meet a supervisor every two weeks | Unlimited rehearsal with an AI supervisor, Reviewer 2 and a viva committee, scored every time | Feedback within seconds instead of weeks; the human mentor's time goes on judgement calls only |
| Pick up the jargon by osmosis | A term bank built from your own reading, drilled aloud, then checked in your real meetings | You say the right word at the right moment because you have already said it 20 times |
| Write at the end | Write from week 1: a paper card a day, a one-page proposal by week 10 | Daily short writing produced far more output than waiting for inspiration in Boice's studies |
| Choose a problem by chance | Choose with Hamming's importance test, Alon's feasibility-versus-interest map and DARPA's Heilmeier questions | Fewer months lost on problems that are too hard, too small, or already solved |
| Present once a year | A 5-minute talk every week and a mock conference every month | Speaking fluently is a trained skill; this is iReliabee's home ground |

One caution from the evidence: deliberate practice explains much less of performance in professional work than in games (Macnamara et al., 2014). So the track also invests in what practice alone cannot give: good problem choice and a real mentor from level 3.

## The ladder

Seven levels, each ending in an exit test scored against a rubric. You move up by passing the test, not by time spent. Weeks assume about 10 hours a week; more hours move you faster.

| Level | Weeks | At the end you can | Exit test |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 Orientation | 1–2 | Explain how research works: the question-to-publication cycle, peer review, venues, preprints, roles in a lab, what "a contribution" means | 10-minute oral with the AI supervisor on how research works, plus 60 starter terms at 90% recall |
| 1 Reader | 3–8 | Read a paper in three passes, write a one-page paper card, and map a field's 30 key papers | Present 3 papers at a journal club and answer the panel's questions; literature map reviewed |
| 2 Question-maker | 9–12 | Find a gap, turn it into a testable question and hypothesis, and defend why it matters | One-page proposal passes all eight Heilmeier questions in a live defence |
| 3 Methodologist | 13–22 | Design a study, choose and justify methods and statistics, handle data reproducibly and ethically | Replicate one published result, with code, data notes and a preregistration-style plan |
| 4 Author | 23–32 | Write a paper people can review: abstract, introduction (CARS), methods, results, limitations; review others' papers | Workshop-length paper or preprint that "Reviewer 2" scores as a weak accept or better, plus two peer reviews written |
| 5 Communicator | runs alongside 2–6 | Give a 10-minute talk and a poster pitch, handle hostile questions, write to a professor, chair a discussion | Mock conference: talk plus 10 minutes of Q&A, scored on clarity, accuracy and composure |
| 6 Independent researcher | 33–52 | Set a research agenda, extend your replication into original work, pitch a grant, mentor a newcomer | Mock viva: 60 minutes with a three-person AI committee on your own project |

Level 5 is not a stage you wait for. Speaking starts in week 1 and gets harder as the content does, because speaking fluently about research is the skill iReliabee exists to train.

## Six skill pillars

Every lesson, drill and test belongs to one pillar. Each pillar leaves an artefact you can show a supervisor, an admissions panel or an employer, so the track builds a portfolio as it teaches.

| Pillar | What you master | Core methods | Artefact it leaves |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Read | Three-pass reading, critical appraisal, synthesis across papers | Keshav's three passes; claim / evidence / limitation cards | Paper cards and a field map of 30 to 100 papers |
| Think | Problem choice, gaps, questions, hypotheses, contribution | Hamming's important problems; Alon's feasibility-versus-interest map; Heilmeier's eight questions | One-page proposals, a research agenda |
| Do | Study design, statistics, data, code, reproducibility, ethics | Lakens on inference; McElreath on causal models; The Turing Way; preregistration on OSF; Missing Semester for tools | A replication repository and a preregistered plan |
| Write | Paper structure, clear sentences, related work, reviewing | Swales' CARS introductions; Whitesides' outline-first; Mensh & Kording's ten rules; Peyton Jones: write to do the research | A preprint draft and two peer reviews |
| Speak | Terms in context, hedging, talks, Q&A, viva, emails to professors | Peyton Jones' research talk; phrasebank moves; iReliabee's live coach and mock panels | Recorded talks with scores, a viva transcript |
| Operate | Tools, integrity, collaboration, authorship, working with a mentor | Zotero, Semantic Scholar, Connected Papers; lab handbooks (Poldrack, Peelle, Aly); UKRIO and ORI on integrity | A working literature setup and a mentor agreement |

## The lab rhythm

The track copies how a good lab runs its week, so a newcomer builds the habits of one before joining one. Home screen shows today's list; each item takes one click to start.

| When | What | Minutes | What the app does |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Daily | Retrieval reps: terms and paper cards due today | 10 | Spaced flashcards; you answer aloud, the app scores what you said |
| Daily | One reading pass on today's paper | 20–40 | Opens the next paper at the right pass; you fill the card as you go |
| Daily | One speaking rep: explain a concept in 60 seconds, or use 3 new terms in an answer | 5 | Scores accuracy, term use, hedging and filler words |
| Weekly | Journal club: present one paper to the panel | 20 | AI panel asks the questions a real journal club would; debrief afterwards |
| Weekly | Lab meeting: a 5-minute progress update to the AI supervisor | 15 | Supervisor probes what you did, what broke and what is next; action items go to your notebook |
| Weekly | Writing sprint | 60 | Short timed session on the current artefact (card, proposal, section) |
| Weekly | Peer review of one paper (from level 3) | 45 | Structured review form; compared with the real public reviews on OpenReview where they exist |
| Monthly | Mock conference and level checkpoint | 45 | 10-minute talk plus Q&A, then the level's mastery report |

When the learner joins a real lab or supervisor, the same routine moves to real meetings: the live coach listens in lab meetings and journal clubs and prompts the right terms, just as it does in interviews today.

## Speaking like a researcher

Sounding like a researcher is knowing four things: the right term, the right strength of claim, the right move for the moment, and when to say "I don't know". The language engine trains all four, in that order.

**1. Terms, in three tiers.** Each term is a card with a plain definition, one example sentence, a common misuse and the moment to use it.

- Core research terms, about 150, the same in every field: hypothesis, construct, operationalise, confound, effect size, statistical power, baseline, ablation, generalisability, threats to validity, preregistration, replication.
- Field terms, built automatically from the papers the learner reads: every term the reading notes mark becomes a card.
- Open-science terms from the FORRT glossary, a peer-reviewed community glossary.

**2. Strength of claim (hedging).** New researchers overclaim or apologise. The engine teaches a five-step ladder and scores which step each sentence uses.

| Evidence you have | Say | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One small study | "These results suggest…" | "This proves…" |
| Several consistent studies | "There is consistent evidence that…" | "Everyone knows…" |
| A clear correlation | "X is associated with Y" | "X causes Y" |
| A null result | "We found no evidence of an effect at this sample size" | "There is no effect" |
| A guess | "One possible explanation, which we have not tested, is…" | Stating the guess as fact |

**3. Moves for each moment.** Phrases are grouped by what you are trying to do, in the style of Manchester's Academic Phrasebank and Swales' CARS model, and practised aloud:

- Establish the territory, show the gap, state your contribution (introductions and pitches).
- Describe a method, report a result, state a limitation.
- Ask a question at a talk: name the part, then ask one thing.
- Answer a hard question: restate it, answer what you can, name what you would need to know the rest.
- Take criticism: "That's a fair point. The design can't rule that out; a follow-up with X would."
- Say "I don't know" well: "I haven't looked at that. My guess is Y, and I'd check it by Z."

**4. Drills.** Say it three ways (plain, precise, hedged). Correct the overclaim. Explain the term to a 12-year-old, then to a reviewer. Use three new terms in a 60-second answer. Every drill is spoken and scored.

**Measured in real life.** In real lab meetings, the live coach counts how often the learner uses their terms correctly and how often their claims are stronger than their evidence, and puts both on the Progress screen.

## The practice panel

Six AI characters stand in for the people a researcher must convince. Each is a Practice scenario (text or voice), reads the learner's own papers, cards and drafts, and scores against the rubric for the current level.

| Character | Plays | Pushes on | Used from level |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The Supervisor | A patient but exacting PI in the learner's field | "What exactly did you do? Why that method? What's next?" | 0 |
| The Journal Club | Three peers who read the same paper | Hidden assumptions, weak baselines, what the figures really show | 1 |
| Reviewer 2 | A sceptical expert reviewer | Novelty, threats to validity, overclaiming, missing related work | 2 |
| The Conference Audience | A mixed room after a talk | Short hard questions, the "so what?", a question outside your area | 2 |
| The Committee | Three examiners at a viva or proposal defence | Depth, alternative explanations, limits, what you would do differently | 4 |
| The Professor you email | A busy academic reading a cold email | Is it short, specific, and clear about why them and what you ask | 5 |

The panel adapts. It asks easier questions after a failed rep and harder ones after a pass, and brings back weak topics a few days later. That is spaced retrieval applied to speaking.

## Measuring mastery

Mastery is shown by evidence, never by time spent or lessons clicked. Each skill (about 40 across the six pillars) carries a score from 0 to 4 that only rises when the learner produces something the rubric can grade.

| Score | Means | Typical evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 Not started | Never attempted | None |
| 1 Aware | Can recognise and define it | Card recall above 80% |
| 2 Guided | Can do it with prompts | Passed a drill with hints |
| 3 Independent | Can do it alone, correctly | Passed a timed drill or panel session unaided |
| 4 Fluent | Can do it under pressure and explain it to others | Passed it in a mock conference, viva or real meeting |

Example rubric, "Present a paper" (journal club), each 1 to 4: states the question and why it matters in 30 seconds; describes the method accurately; reports the main result with its size and uncertainty; names one real limitation; answers two panel questions without overclaiming; uses field terms correctly.

The **Research readiness** view shows the six pillars as a radar, the current level, the next exit test, and the three weakest skills with a one-click drill for each. Scores decay slowly if a skill is not practised, which brings it back into the daily reps.

A human check is built in: at levels 3 and 6 the learner can export the portfolio (cards, proposal, replication, talk recordings, scores) to share with a real mentor for sign-off.

## How it fits the app

Most of the engine already exists. Research Track adds one screen, a curriculum and a mastery model on top, and points the existing features at research.

**One new menu item: Research** (under PREPARE), with six tabs: Today (the daily list), Ladder (levels and exit tests), Papers (paper cards and the field map), Terms (the term bank), Projects (proposal and replication tracker) and Readiness (the radar and weakest skills).

| Existing feature | Its job in the track |
| --- | --- |
| Notebook reading notes (research-paper template, page chips, coverage) | Becomes the paper card: three-pass fields added (question, method, result, limitation, my critique) |
| Study flashcards and quizzes | The spaced-retrieval engine for terms and paper cards |
| Practice: mock conversation, Explain a topic, Check my script | The practice panel characters, the 60-second explanations and talk rehearsal |
| Academic / PhD and thesis-defence coaches | The base for the Supervisor and Committee personas |
| Plans and People research | Preparing for a real supervisor meeting, and researching labs and professors to approach |
| Live companion | Coaching in real lab meetings, journal clubs and conference Q&A |
| Progress | Shows research readiness beside the speaking trends |
| Library | Holds the PDFs the learner reads |

**New data (local SQLite, same as everything else):**

- `research_track`: field, goal (PhD application, first paper, industry research), level, weekly hours.
- `research_skills` and `research_evidence`: each skill's 0–4 score and the drill, test or session that proved it.
- `term_cards`: term, definition, example, misuse, tier, source paper, and spaced-repetition fields (due date, interval, ease).
- `paper_cards`: a link to the reading notebook plus the three-pass fields and the learner's critique.
- `research_projects`: proposal, replication and preprint milestones with links to code and files.

**Curriculum as data.** Levels, lessons, rubrics and the core term list ship as versioned JSON in the app, so content can be improved without code changes, and a field pack (for example speech science or machine learning) can be added as one file.

## Costs, privacy and honest limits

**Credits.** Reading, flashcards and the ladder are free. Anything the AI does uses credits: writing paper cards from a PDF, panel sessions, scoring spoken drills, reviewing drafts. The app shows the cost before each one, as reading notes already do, and the Today list shows the day's expected cost. A typical week at 10 hours should cost roughly the same as two to three hours of live session; the exact figure needs measuring in the beta.

**Privacy.** Before launch, the Privacy Policy must name each new kind of content sent to the AI: the learner's drafts, proposals, peer reviews and spoken drill answers. Papers and transcripts are already covered. This follows the house rule that the policy is updated before a new kind of content is sent.

**What it cannot do.**

- It cannot replace a human mentor. The evidence on PhD students is clear that supervision quality is the biggest factor. The track prepares the learner to find, impress and use a mentor well, and asks for one by level 3.
- AI feedback can be confidently wrong. Every panel answer that cites a fact links to the paper it came from, and the learner is taught to check.
- It cannot run experiments, get ethics approval or grant access to data. Version 1 suits computational and empirical fields (computer science, data science, psychology, speech and language, economics) better than wet-lab sciences.
- It cannot promise to beat anyone. What it can do is remove the slow parts of the normal route. Whether that closes a gap on someone who started years earlier depends on hours, problem choice and luck.
