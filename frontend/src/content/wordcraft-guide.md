# Wordcraft: beginner to expert communicator

Wordcraft is a guided path inside the app that takes anyone from their first English words to speaking and writing like an expert: every word, phrase and sentence learned, used out loud, and checked. It runs on the same daily-session engine as the Research Track, follows the evidence on how languages are actually learned, and measures communication by clarity and effect, never by how fluent someone sounds.

## How it beats the usual way

Most courses drill grammar rules, hand out topic word lists and score how smooth you sound. The research points somewhere else, and Wordcraft follows it.

| The usual way | What Wordcraft does | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Long grammar lessons and drills on their own | A short, clear rule, then using it in sentences that mean something; building longer sentences from short ones | Explicit teaching beats leaving learners to notice ([Norris & Ortega, 2000](https://doi.org/10.1111/0023-8333.00136); [Spada & Tomita, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9922.2010.00562.x)). Stand-alone grammar teaching did not improve writing (effect −0.32); sentence combining did (+0.50) ([Graham & Perin, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.99.3.445)) |
| Topic word lists, read and reread | The most frequent words and whole phrases first, tested and spaced over weeks | Coverage decides understanding ([Nation, 2006](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/nation_2006.pdf)); spacing has a medium-to-large effect ([Kim & Webb, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12479)); practice tests beat rereading ([Dunlosky et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100612453266)) |
| The teacher gives the right answer | You are prompted to fix it yourself first; the answer comes second; only mistakes that block meaning are marked | Prompts led to more self-repair and larger effects than recasts in classrooms ([Lyster & Saito, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0272263109990520)) |
| Aim for a native accent | Aim to be understood: the sounds that change meaning first | A strong accent does not necessarily reduce intelligibility ([Munro & Derwing, 1995](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-1770.1995.tb00963.x)) |
| Score speed, pauses and fillers | Score whether the message landed: content, structure, clarity. Pace and fillers are shown only if you turn them on | Fluency as a goal is considered by some to be ableist; focus on what is said, not how ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/clinical-topics/fluency-disorders/)) |
| Points, leaderboards and prizes | A map, a story, characters, streaks you can protect, and honest feedback on what you did well | Expected rewards undermine motivation; positive feedback helps ([Deci, Koestner & Ryan, 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627)) |

One caution: the evidence on game features is small and mixed ([Sailer & Homner, 2020](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w.pdf)), so they serve the habit, never replace the learning.

## The journey: seven realms

Seven realms take you from first words to expert, each lined up with a CEFR level. A placement check on day one starts you in the right realm, so nobody repeats what they already know.

| Realm | Level | By the end you can | Grammar landmarks | Words known (about) | Study hours from zero |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 The Harbour | Pre-A1 | Greet, say who you are, name everyday things, count | *be*, *this/that*, *a/the*, plurals | 300 | — |
| 1 Market Town | A1 | Ask for and give simple information about daily needs | Present simple, *can*, *there is/are* | under 1,500 | 90–100 |
| 2 The Crossroads | A2 | Describe your past, routines and plans; ask direct questions | Past simple, direct *wh-* questions, comparatives, *going to* | 1,500–2,500 | 180–200 |
| 3 The Bridge | B1 | Tell a story, give opinions with reasons, handle most work and travel talk | Present perfect, indirect questions, first and second conditionals, linking words | 2,750–3,250 | 350–400 |
| 4 The City | B2 | Argue a view, weigh pros and cons, run a meeting | Reported speech, passives, relative clauses, phrasal verbs | 3,250–3,750 | 500–600 |
| 5 The Summit | C1 | Speak and write precisely for work and study; structure talks and reports | Hedging, emphasis (cleft sentences), nominalisation, register | 3,750–4,500 | 700–800 |
| 6 The Grand Hall | C2 | Persuade, explain between people, adapt tone to any audience | Nuance, idiom, rhetoric, mediation | 4,500–5,000+ | 1,000–1,200 |

Word ranges are from [Milton (2010)](https://www.eurosla.org/monographs/EM01/211-232Milton.pdf) and overlap a lot between people; study hours are Cambridge's [guided learning hours](https://support.cambridgeenglish.org/hc/en-gb/articles/202838506-Guided-learning-hours), counted from zero. Question forms follow the order learners actually acquire them: direct questions, then indirect, then reported speech ([Cambridge, 2021](https://www.cambridge.org/elt/blog/2021/06/23/using-cefr-criterial-features-for-grammar-instruction/)). The C1 and C2 landmarks are our choice and still need checking against the English Grammar Profile.

## Six skill pillars

Every lesson builds one of six pillars, and each pillar leaves something you can look back on. Over a week, time is spread across Paul Nation's [four strands](https://www.wgtn.ac.nz/__data/assets/pdf_file/0019/1626121/2007-Four-strands.pdf) (understanding, speaking and writing for meaning, studying the language, and using what you know with ease), with more input at the start.

| Pillar | What you learn | What it leaves behind | Strand |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Words** | The most frequent words first: meaning, use, the words they go with | Your word deck, with how well you hold each word | Studying the language |
| **Phrases** | Whole chunks grouped by job: greeting, asking, agreeing, softening a claim | Your phrasebook | Studying the language |
| **Sentences** | Word order, tenses and questions in the order learners acquire them; joining short sentences into longer ones | Your sentence journal | Studying the language, speaking and writing |
| **Understanding** | Short reading and listening passages at your level, then free courses we link to | Your reading and listening log | Understanding |
| **Speaking** | Saying it out loud: repeating, retelling, answering characters | Your practice rounds and recordings | Speaking, using with ease |
| **Communicating** | Getting a message across: structure, turn-taking, asking for clarity, persuading, explaining between people | Your talks, emails and written pieces | Speaking and writing for meaning |

## The daily adventure

Each day is one guided session of 25 to 40 minutes: press Start, do one step at a time, and finish with "You're done for today". It uses the same engine as the Research Track, so pausing, the streak and Keep going all work the same way.

1. **Words.** Due cards and up to 10 new ones. Say the meaning out loud, turn the card, grade yourself.
2. **Today's quest.** Why this lesson matters, and its words and phrases on cards you flip.
3. **Read or listen.** A short passage at your level, written for Wordcraft, with links to free BBC and British Council material for more.
4. **Practise.** Five to eight exercises the app checks at once: choose the right word, fill the gap, put the words in order, fix the mistake, join two sentences into one. A wrong answer first gets a hint; the answer and the reason come second.
5. **Write.** One sentence to a short paragraph using today's pattern. From realm 2 on, the Editor reads it and says what works and one thing to change.
6. **Say it.** Speak on today's topic, with no clock pushing you; or talk to the realm's character. Retell the same story twice: it gets easier, which is the point.
7. **Check.** Can you explain today's point? "Not yet" sends you back over the passage and the exercises.

Once a week a **mission** asks you to use the week's phrases in real life: in a real call the app coaches, in an email, or with a character. When a realm's lessons are done, its **exit test** becomes the last step of the day.

## Every word, phrase and sentence

Wordcraft teaches words, phrases and sentence patterns as three kinds of card, and builds sentences with five kinds of exercise.

**Cards.** Every card has a plain-English meaning, an example sentence, the words it usually goes with, and a note on how people really use it.

| Card | Example | Taught how |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Word | *decide*: choose after thinking. Goes with: *decide to*, *decide on*, *hard to decide* | Most frequent first; new words taught in a block, mixed in later reviews ([Brunmair & Richter, 2019](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000209)) |
| Phrase | *Could you say that again?* (asking for clarity) | Learned whole, grouped by the job it does; chunks make speaking easier to plan ([Boers & Lindstromberg, 2012](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0267190512000050)) |
| Pattern | *Although \_\_\_, \_\_\_.* (a contrast inside one sentence) | A short rule, then built, fixed and spoken |

**Exercises, checked on your computer at once:**

- **Choose** the word or phrase that fits, and see why the others don't.
- **Fill the gap** in a real sentence.
- **Put in order:** tap scrambled words into a sentence.
- **Fix it:** one mistake that changes meaning; you rewrite the sentence.
- **Join them:** two or three short sentences become one, in more than one correct way.

**Feedback** prompts before it tells: a hint first, then the answer and the rule. It marks only mistakes that block meaning, and never treats a repeated sound or a pause as an error.

**Your own words.** You can add words and phrases you meet anywhere as your own cards.

## Characters you practise with

Eight AI characters give you someone real to talk to at every realm, typed or by voice. Each one speaks at your realm's level, uses the words you are learning, and scores what the realm's exit asks for.

| Character | Who they are | You practise | Realms |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **The Guide** | A patient tutor who travels with you | Explaining what you meant; asking about words; fixing your own mistakes | All |
| **The Shopkeeper** | Runs a busy market stall | Asking, buying, numbers, polite requests | 1–2 |
| **The Neighbour** | New in town and curious about you | Small talk, your life, likes and plans | 1–3 |
| **The Colleague** | Works with you and needs things done | Updates, asking for help, explaining a problem | 3–4 |
| **The Debater** | Always takes the other side, politely | Opinions, reasons, disagreeing without offence | 4–5 |
| **The Interviewer** | Hires for a job you want | Telling your story with evidence, handling hard questions | 3–6 |
| **The Audience** | A room after your talk | Presenting one clear message, answering questions | 5–6 |
| **The Editor** | Reads everything you write | Sentences, paragraphs, emails and essays, with clear notes | All |

## Measuring mastery

A realm is mastered when the evidence agrees; how smooth you sound is never part of it.

**Day one: placement.** Quick questions that get harder, written for Wordcraft. The result is a starting realm; the realms below it stay open for review, and their words come back now and then. You can also rate yourself on each realm's "I can" statements in Progress; self-ratings are least reliable for skills you have not used, so the questions carry more weight ([Ross, 1998](https://doi.org/10.1177/026553229801500101)).

**The realm exit:**

| Evidence | Pass mark | How it is measured |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lessons | All six finished | The daily session |
| Words and phrases | Hold 85% of the realm's cards | Your review history |
| Exercises | 80% right first time across the realm's lessons | Checked on your computer |
| A conversation | 7/10 or more with the realm's character | Scored on content, structure and clarity: did the message land? |
| A written piece (realm 2 on) | 7/10 or more from the Editor | Task done, organisation, range, accuracy that affects meaning |

**Progress over time**, shown as trends, never as "your level": cards held, word variety in your writing (MTLD, which does not depend on length; [McCarthy & Jarvis, 2010](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/BRM.42.2.381.pdf)), sentence length and joining, the Editor's scores, and your "I can" ratings. Scores in practice are not proof of learning ([Bjork & Bjork, 2011](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/04/EBjork_RBjork_2011.pdf)), which is why the exit mixes spaced recall with new tasks.

**Pace and fillers** stay off unless you turn them on, and are never a goal. Cutting fillers can push people who stutter into swapping or avoiding words ([ASHA](https://www.asha.org/practice-portal/clinical-topics/fluency-disorders/)). Saying "I stutter" at the start of a conversation is practised as a skill; listeners rate speakers who say so as more friendly and confident ([Byrd et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1044/2016_AJSLP-15-0164)).

## Fun that works, and the tricks we leave out

The fun is there to build a daily habit and a sense of getting better; it is not where the learning happens. Game features help a little on average, and a story helps most ([Sailer & Homner, 2020](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w.pdf)); what keeps people going is feeling able, choosing for yourself and feeling connected ([Ryan & Deci, 2000](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_SDT.pdf)).

| In | Why |
| --- | --- |
| An illustrated map of seven realms, with your marker moving along it | A story and a visible journey |
| Characters who know your level and today's words | Feeling connected |
| A streak, with one free rest day a week that keeps it alive | Duolingo found streak protection kept more learners ([blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak)); a broken streak can demotivate |
| Choosing which character to talk to, and practising any lesson any time | Choice |
| Specific praise: what you did well, then one thing to try | Positive feedback raises motivation ([Deci et al., 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627)) |
| A celebration at the end of each day and each realm | Marking real progress |

| Left out | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Leaderboards | They rank people against each other; the low-ranked lose heart |
| Points to spend and prizes | Expected rewards undermine motivation ([Deci et al., 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627)) |
| "Lives" lost for mistakes | Mistakes are how you learn; nothing punishes them |
| Guilt-trip reminders | Pressure, not choice |
| Speed timers on speaking | Speed pressure works against users who stutter |

## How it fits the app

Most of Wordcraft is parts the app already has, pointed at a new curriculum. **One new menu item, Wordcraft**, opens it; its tabs mirror Research: Guide, Map, Today, Realms, Cards, Writing, Missions and Progress.

| Already in the app | What Wordcraft uses it for |
| --- | --- |

| Term cards with spaced review | Word, phrase and pattern cards, with the same schedule and grades |
| Practice partners, typed or by voice | The characters you talk to in each realm |
| Speech measures: pace, fillers, talk share | Shown for awareness only; never part of a pass mark |

| Export and import | Carries your progress to another computer |

## Costs, privacy and limits

**Credits.** Cards, lessons, exercises, placement, the map and progress all run on your computer and cost nothing. Talking to a character costs the same credits as any practice round. An Editor check is one short AI call, charged like other text jobs.

**Privacy.** The Privacy Policy lists Wordcraft's writing and practice answers among the content that can be sent to the AI, including DeepSeek on the text tiers. Audio is never sent for Wordcraft; speaking steps you do alone stay on your computer.

**What it cannot do yet:**

- It is not a certified test; its CEFR alignment is our own estimate, checked against published profiles.
- It does not score pronunciation from audio.
- Levels C1 and C2 lean on AI characters and the Editor more than on fixed exercises.
- A human teacher's judgement is still better than any score here.
